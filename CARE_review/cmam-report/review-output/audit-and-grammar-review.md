# Audit and grammar review: Treating Child Wasting at Scale

Reviewed 28 September 2026. Source: the pasted Claude draft supplied by the user.

The draft has a useful structure and mostly sound grammar, but needs substantive revision before circulation. Its principal weakness is moving too quickly from study findings to programme recommendations. Several numbers are accurately reproduced but lack essential information about denominators, populations or follow-up periods.

This is an editorial and targeted evidence audit, not a systematic review or a formal GRADE assessment. I checked the principal recommendations and selected numerical claims against accessible publications, WHO guidance and programme sources. I have not independently reproduced every calculation, reviewed every supplement, or verified private correspondence. The accompanying edited draft incorporates the corrections below; bracketed editorial queries identify remaining source or programme information gaps.

## Corrections that matter most

### 1. Define the age range and avoid presenting MUAC as the only diagnostic criterion

The opening moves from all children under five to MUAC thresholds without specifying that the stated thresholds concern children aged 6–59 months. It also omits weight-for-height/length criteria. CMAM is a package of assessment, nutritional treatment, medical care and referral; the opening makes it sound like a uniform RUTF regimen for every child with wasting.

**Action:** Specify the target age range, distinguish outpatient eligibility from severe anthropometric status, and include the alternative diagnostic route. Use “bilateral pitting oedema”, not an unspecified swelling. Infants under six months require a separate pathway. [WHO guideline](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK601655/).

### 2. Remove the unsupported inference about CMAM's “true” benefit

“CMAM has never been trialled against no treatment … so its measured benefit understates its true one” does not follow. A comparison with alternative diets estimates a different effect from a comparison with no care. The absence of an untreated control does not establish the size of an underestimate.

**Replacement:** “These trials compare RUTF with other dietary approaches; they do not estimate the effect of the full CMAM package compared with no treatment.” The reported recovery RR of 1.33 is supported, but the review does not establish a mortality benefit for that comparison. [Schoonees et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31090070/).

Also replace “The binding constraint is therefore not efficacy” with “Coverage, retention and sustained recovery are major implementation priorities.” Calling something *the binding constraint* requires more evidence about the proposed setting and programme.

### 3. Correct study-design descriptions and the overall evidence rating

The Burundi comparison uses observational screening data; “two trials” is incorrect. Replace it with “two studies” and identify the Burundi design. Its 98% versus 8% result should not be presented as a randomised estimate of changing the screening location. [Odjidja et al.](https://doi.org/10.1093/inthealth/ihz119).

The Kita article describes randomisation, but there was only one cluster per arm. The 2023 Cochrane review therefore analysed it with non-randomised studies. Add this qualification instead of treating it as an ordinary multi-cluster RCT. [Cochrane review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10467022/).

The summary label “Strong — several randomised trials” is consequently too broad. Use “Evidence varies by intervention and outcome; includes randomised and observational studies.” WHO's recommendation for community health workers is conditional and based on very-low-certainty evidence. The newer Gao trial adds evidence, but does not by itself justify a blanket certainty upgrade. [WHO recommendation B17](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK601655/table/ch4.tab36/).

### 4. Clarify the ComPAS denominators and food comparison

The 76.3% versus 73.5% recovery figures are **per-protocol** results, not the proportion of all enrolled children who recovered. The paper reports 981/1,286 versus 884/1,202 in that analysis; 4,110 children were enrolled overall. The quoted 1.8% mortality values also use the reported per-protocol denominators. Mixing these values with enrolment totals and defaulting percentages without labels makes the programme appear easier to compare than it is.

The 122 versus 193 sachets comparison concerns children admitted with SAM who reached recovery. Standard-arm food included **RUTF and RUSF**, not only RUTF. Describe the severe-case defaulting percentages as unadjusted subgroup figures; the paper does not establish a general reduction in defaulting across all secondary analyses. [Bailey et al.](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1003192).

### 5. Correct the Taimaka recovery qualification

The 92.9% figure is not merely self-reported: **it excludes non-response cases**. GiveWell explicitly reports this exclusion and shows the calculation. Add it wherever that percentage appears. Do not compare it directly with trial recovery rates using different denominators.

Also change “A programme already operating this model” to “A related delivery model”. The cited account describes Taimaka employing staff within government facilities; that is not identical to the Gao intervention's additional community treatment sites. The US$105 and approximately 9× cash estimates are modelled, dated estimates, not experimental comparisons. [GiveWell's assessment](https://www.givewell.org/research/grants/taimaka-acute-malnutrition-treatment-in-gombe-state-nigeria-november-2024-and-program-monitoring-march-2025).

### 6. Do not treat all simplified protocols as identical

“Set the RUTF dose by MUAC band rather than by body weight” describes ComPAS more closely than the whole class. OptiMA uses both MUAC and weight in dosing; MANGO is a reduced-dose trial rather than a full test of the combined SAM/MAM model.

**Replacement:** “Simplified approaches change different parts of treatment, including admission criteria, products, dosing and follow-up. ComPAS uses fixed sachet quantities by MUAC category, whereas OptiMA adjusts dosing using MUAC and body weight.” [Niger trial](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajcnut.2025.07.004).

### 7. Clarify the Niger trial's populations and follow-up clock

The primary analysis included 1,732 children; the severe-case analysis included 1,140 children, with recruitment extending beyond the first population. Do not imply that all 1,140 were a subset of 1,732.

The six-month endpoint is measured **from enrolment**, not six months after discharge. The 50% and 32% food reductions belong to the severe-case comparison and should not be attached without qualification to the full primary population.

“Reinforced follow-up” is a plausible response to attendance difficulties, but was not randomised as a remedy in this trial. The study cannot establish that it would remove the recovery disadvantage. Replace the recommendation with a monitored, locally authorised protocol choice and evaluation of attendance support. [Daures et al., trial report](https://horizon.documentation.ird.fr/exl-doc/pleins_textes/2025-11/010095394.pdf).

### 8. Separate uncomplicated status from severity

“Simplified protocol for the uncomplicated caseload; standard protocol … for MUAC below 115 mm or oedema” creates overlapping categories. A child can have uncomplicated SAM and be eligible for outpatient care.

**Action:** State which children are eligible for outpatient treatment, then specify the protocol for the severe and moderate groups within that population. Avoid presenting severe anthropometry and medical complications as interchangeable.

### 9. Soften the interpretation of Gao and its costs

The severe-case recovery percentages are correctly reproduced. However, “decentralisation and simplification work together” sounds like proof of interaction. The three-arm study did not include a facility-only simplified arm, so it cannot establish that both components must be introduced together in every setting. The unadjusted difference between the two community-inclusive arms is 16.1 **percentage points**, not “16 percentage points” as an adjusted causal effect. [López-Ejeda et al.](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2024.1283148).

The Gao cost table is supported. Generalisations are not: “decentralisation lowers cost per child only if it raises the number treated”, “budget supervision as the largest cost line”, and “simplification contributes mainly to recovery and retention rather than to cost” extend one evaluation too far. Coordination **and** supervision were combined; local cost structures and capacity can differ. US$179 versus US$210 per child treated also shows that simplified care was not cheaper on every metric. [Cichon et al.](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-025-21411-5).

The ComPAS fixed-budget calculation is arithmetically reasonable: 1,041/918 − 1 = 13.4%. Present it as illustrative and conditional on unit costs remaining constant, not a demonstrated scale-up result.

### 10. Recast stricter discharge criteria as an option to evaluate

The draft turns predictors into interventions. An association between larger MUAC at discharge and lower subsequent relapse does not demonstrate that extending treatment until a higher threshold causes the same reduction. Even predictors analysed within a randomised trial are not themselves randomised interventions.

Replace “each additional millimetre … reduced the hazard” with “each additional millimetre … was associated with a 23% lower hazard”. Delete “this lever is inexpensive to change”: changing a rule may be administratively simple, while implementing it can substantially increase treatment duration and resource use. Replace “best-supported lever” with “a candidate for evaluation”. [Kangas et al.](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu15112636).

A relevant update is missing: Guesdon et al. (2026) pooled 21 studies with 15,394 children and found higher SAM relapse among those still meeting MAM criteria at discharge. This strengthens the prognostic case for adequate recovery, but remains an analysis of observed discharge status, not a randomised test of a higher threshold. [Guesdon et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajcnut.2026.101264).

### 11. Update the caregiver-screening paragraph

Bountogo et al. is a completed, published feasibility RCT with results, not simply evidence that a study has been completed. The primary time-to-relapse-detection estimate was inconclusive (HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.38–1.12). Hospitalisation/death was lower in an exploratory secondary comparison (3% versus 14%), which warrants confirmation.

Replace “The approach does not prevent relapse” with “Its intended role is earlier detection and referral; its effects on relapse and other outcomes need further evaluation.” Do not state that earlier detection was demonstrated. Add a registry link before retaining the claim that a larger trial is registered. [Bountogo et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41569394/).

### 12. Avoid claiming that unmeasured outcomes showed no effect

“Two approaches have been tested without effect on relapse or nutritional status” is inaccurate when the next bullet says psychosocial trials did not measure relapse.

**Replacement:** “These studies have not established a consistent improvement in nutritional outcomes. The psychosocial-stimulation trials did not assess relapse.” The absence of a significant difference is not proof of no effect. Similarly, qualify the statement that no post-discharge intervention has addressed illness: the draft has not established the absence of all relevant evidence. [Bliznashka et al.](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.15077).

### 13. Qualify the coverage, relapse and burden estimates

The 42.8 million, 6.6% and 12.2 million estimates agree with UNICEF's 2025 JME. Specify that these are prevalence-based estimates, not all episodes occurring during 2024. [UNICEF](https://data.unicef.org/topic/nutrition/malnutrition/).

The 38.3% coverage finding and 84%/55% barrier summaries are supported. However, the 44 assessments are from 2012–2013 and are not a representative current global estimate. Selection bias means coverage elsewhere **may** have been lower; it does not prove that “the true figure is lower”. Delete “largest” unless explicitly limited to the source's publication date. [Rogers et al.](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0128666).

The 22–63% relapse range is supported for the three study settings and refers to acute malnutrition, not solely SAM. The comparison group had no episode in the preceding year, not necessarily no lifetime history. Replace “never-malnourished neighbours” accordingly. [King et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39706667/).

The mortality estimate is supported, but the comparator is specifically WHZ ≥ −1. “Normal weight-for-height” is less precise. [Olofin et al.](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0064636).

### 14. Separate historical policy observations from present permission

Dalglish et al.'s account of emergency use does not establish that the proposed programme can now begin under an emergency provision. Replace that sentence with a requirement to check the current national pathway for the selected setting. [Dalglish et al.](https://doi.org/10.1111/mcn.12920).

Replace “ahead of WHO guidance” with “differs from WHO's 2023 recommendations”. “Ahead” implies that a change is an improvement or likely future standard.

## Grammar and editorial changes

There are few outright grammatical errors. Most improvements concern precision, sentence structure and consistency.

| Original | Suggested wording | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| “Evidence for a ScaleWorks programme on…” | “Evidence to inform a ScaleWorks programme for…” | Does not imply that the review has already endorsed a programme. |
| “on 2019 admissions data” | “based on 2019 admissions data” | More natural construction; verify the data year. |
| “between 22% and 63% … relapse” | “In the three study settings, 22–63% … relapsed” | Appropriate tense and scope. |
| “two trials show” | “two studies illustrate” | Correct design and inference. |
| “made no difference” | “showed no clear improvement” | Avoids treating a null result as equivalence. |
| “raised it by 16 percentage points” | “had an unadjusted recovery proportion 16.1 percentage points higher” | Labels the statistic. |
| “The same providers with and without…” | “The two arms that included community health workers…” | Does not imply identical individual providers. |
| “Different RUTF formulations recover children equally well” | “The review found little or no difference in recovery between standard and alternative formulations” | Natural wording; not proof of exact equality. |
| “a weight-for-age z-score below −3 … tripled it” | “a weight-for-age z-score below −3 was associated with approximately three times the hazard” | Clearer antecedent and non-causal wording. |
| “Relapse occurring regardless” | “Risk of relapse after discharge” | Avoids fatalistic and imprecise phrasing. |
| “Retreating … costs … the initial recovery” | “The cost of initial treatment plus retreatment should be distinguished from the additional cost of retreatment alone” | Correct the economic comparison before final wording. |
| “on both analyses” | “in both analyses” | More idiomatic. |
| “per-protocol analysis” / “intention-to-treat analysis” | Keep these forms consistently | Standard hyphenation. |
| “under 12 months” | “among children younger than 12 months” | Clearer population. |
| “No difference in mortality” | “No statistically significant difference in mortality was detected” | Does not imply proven equivalence. |
| “What has not worked” | “Interventions without established nutritional benefit” | Reflects the evidence. |
| “cost per DALY averted” | Define “disability-adjusted life-year (DALY)” at first use | Accessibility for non-specialist readers. |
| “$”, “US$”, “US$40” | Use “US$” consistently, with a price year where available | Cross-study comparability. |

Keep British English: programme, randomised, enrolment, oedema and standardised. Preserve the published spelling in article titles. Use “recovery” in narrative prose, retaining “cure” only where it is the source's reported metric. Explain CHW, RUSF, RR, HR, aHR, CI and DALY at first use or in a compact glossary. The apparent broken tables in the pasted text may be a copy-and-paste artefact; they have been reconstructed in the edited version.

## Remaining source and commissioning queries

These items are unresolved, not established errors:

- Complete section 1.2 with the actual geography, population, timeframe, budget and intended government partner. The draft cannot yet establish that its proposals are affordable or transferable to ScaleWorks' setting.
- Identify the date and source of Save the Children's feedback; this private input was not supplied.
- Check whether the FAO et al. (2020) document actually bases the one-third estimate on **2019** admissions. Keep it explicitly historical.
- Provide a dated World Bank data extract for Nigeria 11.6% (2021) and Niger 10.9% (2022). Confirm that both are comparable survey estimates, not differently defined indicators.
- Trace the Gombe approximately 1% claim to its precise population and model input. The linked GiveWell page supports low coverage but the exact 1% value was not verified in this audit.
- Check Puett et al.'s tables before retaining “retreating … costs 1.7–2.2 times the initial recovery”. Distinguish **total initial treatment plus retreatment** from **additional retreatment cost**. The paper exists; the draft's wording is not sufficiently explicit. Also check the denominator and assumptions behind the US$2–45 estimate. [Puett et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cdnut.2025.107616).
- In the cash-transfer paragraph, distinguish US$240 in household transfers from total delivery cost and from a universally applicable cost per child. The trial supports the intervention, but the local transfer size and delivery costs need a programme budget. The listed hazard ratios are supported. [Grellety et al.](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-017-0848-y).
- The Park/Zhao/Ostrowski working-paper title and public results status were not independently verified. The registry page was inaccessible during this audit. A registry entry is not itself a working paper; obtain the paper or cite the registry with its exact title, status and access date. Do not label it fabricated on this evidence.
- Add a search date, selection method and limitations paragraph. Label the report a narrative evidence review unless a systematic search and selection process was actually used.
- Complete and standardise references, particularly the two GiveWell dates, the undated working paper and the World Bank database entry. Preserve original article titles and add persistent links.

The edited draft retains explicit editorial queries for unresolved material. It is ready for author review, not for publication with those queries still present.
