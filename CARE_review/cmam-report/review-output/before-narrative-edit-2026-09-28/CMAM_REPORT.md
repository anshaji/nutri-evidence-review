*About this review.* This is a narrative evidence review, not a systematic review. It draws on CEGA's
evidence-synthesis pipeline for community-based management of acute malnutrition — 239 on-topic records,
including 32 randomised trials — supplemented by targeted searches for recent trials, guidelines and
costings, conducted in August and September 2026. Every figure cited has been checked against its
source. Certainty ratings are those assigned by the source authors or by WHO; no new GRADE assessment was
performed.

**Abbreviations:** SAM, severe acute malnutrition; MAM, moderate acute malnutrition; CHW, community health
worker; RCT, randomised controlled trial; RUSF, ready-to-use supplementary food; RR, risk ratio; HR, hazard
ratio; aHR, adjusted hazard ratio; CI, confidence interval; DALY, disability-adjusted life-year.

# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Community-based management of acute malnutrition

Community-based management of acute malnutrition (CMAM) combines community identification, outpatient
nutritional and medical care, and referral for inpatient treatment when needed. This review concerns
children aged 6–59 months. Children with uncomplicated severe wasting who are eligible for outpatient care
receive ready-to-use therapeutic food (RUTF) to eat at home and attend regular follow-up visits; children
with medical complications, poor appetite or severe nutritional oedema may need inpatient care. Severe
wasting is identified by a mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) below 115 mm and/or a weight-for-height or
weight-for-length z-score below −3; MUAC from 115 mm to below 125 mm indicates moderate wasting. Bilateral
pitting oedema is a separate criterion for severe acute malnutrition. Infants younger than six months
follow a different pathway (WHO 2023).

**The scale of the problem.** In 2024, an estimated 42.8 million children under five were wasted — 6.6% of
the age group — of whom 12.2 million were severely wasted (UNICEF, WHO and World Bank 2025). These are
prevalence estimates, not counts of every episode during the year. Severe wasting is associated with a
substantially higher risk of death: in a pooled analysis of ten cohorts covering 53,809 children, severely
wasted children died at 11.6 times the rate of children with a weight-for-height z-score of at least −1
(Olofin et al. 2013).

**Treatment works.** Ready-to-use therapeutic food probably improves recovery compared with alternative
dietary approaches (RR 1.33, 95% CI 1.16–1.54; moderate-certainty evidence) (Schoonees et al. 2019). These
trials compare RUTF with other diets; they do not estimate the effect of the CMAM package against no
treatment.

**Coverage remains low.** The 2020 Global Action Plan on Child Wasting reported that roughly one severely
wasted child in three received treatment (FAO et al. 2020). A review of 44 coverage assessments in 21
countries, conducted in 2012–2013, found mean coverage of 38.3%; its authors noted possible selection
towards better-performing programmes, so coverage elsewhere may have been lower (Rogers et al. 2015).
Neither figure is a current, representative global estimate.

**Recovery is often not sustained.** In three study settings, 22–63% of children discharged as recovered
relapsed to acute malnutrition — moderate or severe — within six months (King et al. 2025).

In technical feedback on the initial review, Save the Children described adherence and coverage, rather
than efficacy, as the binding constraint, and noted that CMAM is coverage-limited (IA partner feedback,
July 2026). The evidence above is consistent with that framing: children are missed before treatment,
discontinue during it, or relapse after discharge. This report is organised around those three stages.

## 1.2 What ScaleWorks is trying to do

> **[To be completed with CARE.]** ScaleWorks aims to design a CMAM programme that governments can adopt and
> that can be scaled beyond its first setting. This section needs ScaleWorks' target geography, population,
> time frame, budget envelope and intended government partner.

Three features of the evidence bear directly on that aim.

**The gap is largest where burden is high and coverage low.** National survey estimates put wasting
prevalence at 11.6% in Nigeria in 2021 and 10.9% in Niger in 2022 (World Bank, accessed September 2026).
GiveWell's cost-effectiveness analysis of treatment in Gombe State, northeastern Nigeria, uses a baseline
treatment coverage of about 1% (GiveWell 2024).

**The evidence base is regional.** The delivery and protocol trials highlighted below were conducted
mainly in Mali, Niger, Kenya, South Sudan and Burkina Faso, with further evidence from Bangladesh, Burundi,
the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Pakistan. Applying them elsewhere requires an assessment of
health-system capacity, population characteristics, treatment practice and caregiver constraints.

**National adoption pathways need local assessment.** A 2020 study of decision-making in Niger, Nigeria,
Somalia and South Sudan found that routine national guidelines generally did not incorporate the combined
protocol, although some elements had been accepted for emergency use (Dalglish et al. 2020). That suggests
a possible route for discussion with ministries; it does not establish current permission in ScaleWorks'
eventual setting.

# 2. What CARE and ScaleWorks should consider

The interventions below follow the three stages: reaching treatment, treatment itself, and sustaining
recovery. For each, the evidence is given with its study design, and the implications for ScaleWorks are
drawn out separately.

## 2.1 Getting children into treatment

**Why children are missed.** When caregivers of untreated children across 44 coverage assessments were
asked why their child was not in the programme, five barriers accounted for 84% of answers. Ranked by
frequency (Rogers et al. 2015):

1. Not recognising that the child was malnourished
2. Not knowing the programme existed
3. High opportunity costs — travel, lost income, lost agricultural labour
4. The child attending a health facility for another reason, such as malaria, without being screened
5. The child having been screened before and turned away as ineligible

The first two — awareness — made up 55% of all reasons given.

**Finding children is not the same as treating them.** Two studies illustrate why better case-finding need
not translate into higher treatment coverage.

- In an observational comparison in Burundi, clinic-based systematic screening enrolled 98% of the cases
  it detected, against 8% for community mass screening: a child identified in a village still has to reach
  a facility (Odjidja et al. 2022).
- In a cluster RCT in Mali, adding preventive supplements to community screening raised screening coverage
  by 40 percentage points but did not change treatment coverage; across both arms, 7.6% of acutely
  malnourished children received appropriate treatment in the month before the endline survey. Volunteers
  gave identified children preventive supplements rather than referring them, and the longitudinal
  analysis found treatment enrolment 10 percentage points lower (Huybregts et al. 2019).

**Decentralising treatment to community health workers.** Three studies evaluate bringing treatment, not
only screening, closer to communities.

| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Kita, Mali (Alvarez Morán et al. 2018) | CHWs and facilities vs facilities only; one cluster per arm, so analysed as non-randomised in the 2023 Cochrane review | Coverage rose from 43.9% to **86.7%**, against 41.6% in the control area; cure 94.2% vs 88.6%; defaulting **4.5% vs 10.8%** (RR 0.42, 95% CI 0.25–0.71); no statistically significant difference in mortality |
| Gao, Mali (López-Ejeda et al. 2024) | Three-arm cluster RCT, 2,038 children | Recovery 76.3% (facilities), 81.8% (+ CHWs, standard protocol), **92.9%** (+ CHWs, simplified protocol). Severe cases: 71.4%, 71.5%, **87.6%** |
| Sindh, Pakistan (Rogers et al. 2019) | Cluster RCT: lady health workers alongside facilities vs facilities only | US$291 vs US$301 per child treated; US$382 vs US$363 per child recovered; recovery 76.0% vs 83.0%. The authors report "considerable uncertainty" over which is more cost-effective |

In Gao, severe-case recovery was similar in the facility-only arm and the arm adding community health
workers under the standard protocol. It was 16.1 percentage points higher, unadjusted, in the arm combining
community health workers with a simplified protocol. This supports evaluating the two changes together;
because the trial had no facility-only simplified arm, it does not establish that they must always be
introduced together.

**Costs in the Gao setting.** An economic evaluation of the Gao trial (Cichon et al. 2025):

| Metric | Facilities only | + CHWs, standard | + CHWs, simplified |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost per child treated | US$272 | US$179 | US$210 |
| Cost per child cured | US$356 | US$219 | US$226 |
| Cost per DALY averted | US$173 | US$60 | US$53 |

Total programme cost was similar across the three arms. Coordination and supervision together accounted
for 42–44% of total cost, and treating more children spread that cost across a larger caseload. ScaleWorks'
budget should rest on local staffing, transport, supervision and caseload assumptions; these figures do not
establish a universal cost structure.

**A related delivery model.** Taimaka provides outpatient treatment through government primary health
facilities in Gombe State, Nigeria, using community health extension workers as triage officers,
app-guided assessment, MUAC-based admission and nurse supervision, with referral for inpatient care. This
differs from Gao's additional community treatment sites. GiveWell's cost-effectiveness model estimates a
cost of about US$105 per child treated and cost-effectiveness roughly nine times its cash-transfer
benchmark; these are model estimates, not trial results. GiveWell reports Taimaka's 2024 recovery rate as
92.9% after excluding non-response from the denominator — about 84% of discharges were recoveries — and
the figure is self-reported. GiveWell's reservations include effects on other health services and on
quality of care (GiveWell 2024).

**Guideline position.** WHO's 2023 guideline states that community health workers can assess, classify and
manage or refer children with wasting, given adequate training and supervision — a conditional
recommendation on very-low-certainty evidence (WHO 2023). A 2023 Cochrane review likewise rated the
evidence on CHW-delivered treatment as very low certainty for recovery, coverage and mortality
(Papadopoulou et al. 2023); the Gao trial was published afterwards.

**Implications for ScaleWorks**

- Screen every child who attends a health facility, whatever the reason for the visit.
- Decentralise treatment, not only screening: every screening point needs a functioning route to
  treatment.
- Align screening, referral and admission criteria with the nationally authorised protocol, including
  routes for children identified by oedema or weight-for-height, and monitor why referred children are not
  enrolled.
- Report case-finding and treatment coverage as separate indicators.
- Budget for training, coordination, supervision and referral, and evaluate decentralisation together with
  protocol simplification.

## 2.2 Treating children: simplified or standard protocol

**What a simplified protocol changes.** Simplified approaches change different elements of care. Combined
protocols treat severe and moderate wasting within one programme using one product, but their admission,
discharge and dosing rules differ. ComPAS sets fixed sachet quantities by MUAC category; OptiMA doses by
body weight within MUAC bands. Reduced-dose trials such as MANGO test one component rather than a combined
programme. Under ComPAS, children with MUAC below 115 mm or oedema receive two sachets a day (about 1,000
kcal); children with MUAC from 115 mm to below 125 mm receive one (about 500 kcal); the dose steps down
once MUAC reaches 115 mm without oedema on two consecutive visits; and children are discharged at 125 mm or
above, without oedema, on two consecutive visits (Bailey et al. 2020).

**The evidence**

| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| ComPAS (Bailey et al. 2020) | Cluster RCT, 4,110 children, Kenya and South Sudan | Per-protocol recovery **76.3% vs 73.5%** — non-inferior; per-protocol mortality 1.8% in both arms. Unadjusted defaulting among severe cases 33.8% vs 44.3%. Among recovered children admitted with SAM, 122 vs 193 sachets of ready-to-use food (the standard arm used both RUTF and RUSF). **US$918 vs US$1,041** per child recovered |
| OptiMA–ComPAS (Daures et al. 2025) | Individually randomised, Niger; 1,732 children in the primary analysis and 1,140 in the severe-case analysis, with continued enrolment to reach it | Primary outcome at six months after enrolment: alive, meeting the study's nutritional criteria and without a further episode. ComPAS non-inferior in intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses; OptiMA in intention-to-treat only. In the severe-case analysis, ComPAS and OptiMA used about 50% and 32% less RUTF respectively. No statistically significant difference in mortality |
| MANGO (Kangas et al. 2019) | RCT, Burkina Faso | Reduced RUTF dose non-inferior on weight gain, with a small negative effect on height gain (0.2 mm/week), more pronounced among children younger than 12 months |

At the ComPAS unit costs, a fixed budget would fund about 13% more recoveries (US$1,041 ÷ US$918 − 1),
if costs held constant as the programme expanded. This is an illustrative calculation, not an observed
effect of scale-up. The Niger endpoint is measured six months after enrolment, not six months after
discharge.

**Where the standard protocol did better.** In the Niger severe-case analysis, the standard protocol
achieved higher nutritional recovery in both intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses. The authors
attribute the difference mainly to attendance: missed visits were more frequent in the simplified arms.
Better follow-up is a plausible response, but it was not tested (Daures et al. 2025).

**What simplification saves depends on the setting.** In Gao, the community arm using the simplified
protocol cost more per child treated than the community arm using the standard protocol (US$210 vs
US$179), while cost per child recovered was similar (US$226 vs US$219). RUTF costs for severe cases were
US$5.70 lower per child under the simplified protocol, and food was a relatively small share of total cost
(Cichon et al. 2025). Lower food use does not translate proportionately into lower programme cost;
ScaleWorks should model the whole programme.

**Formulation matters for relapse.** The Cochrane review found little or no difference in recovery between
standard and alternative RUTF formulations (RR 1.03, 95% CI 0.99–1.08; high-certainty evidence), but
standard RUTF reduced relapse compared with the alternative formulations assessed (RR 0.84, 95% CI
0.72–0.98; high-certainty evidence) (Schoonees et al. 2019).

**Guideline position.** WHO's 2023 guideline retains RUTF dosing indexed to body weight — 150–185
kcal/kg/day, with an option to step down to 100–130 kcal/kg/day once severe wasting and oedema have
resolved — and discharge on both weight-for-height and MUAC (WHO 2023). A fixed-dose, MUAC-only protocol
therefore differs from WHO's 2023 recommendations on dosing and discharge. Pooled observational evidence
also suggests that discharge on MUAC alone can release children still wasted by weight-for-height, who
relapse more often (section 2.3).

**Implications for ScaleWorks**

- Adopt a specified simplified protocol for eligible outpatients, subject to national authorisation and
  local assessment of effectiveness, acceptability and feasibility.
- Specify the protocol for children with uncomplicated severe wasting separately from that for moderate
  wasting, taking the Niger severe-case findings into account.
- If simplifying for severely wasted children, strengthen and monitor attendance, and evaluate whether that
  closes the recovery gap.
- Where weight-for-height can be measured, evaluate checking it before discharge (section 2.3).
- Discuss national guidance, authorisation and evaluation requirements with ministries at the outset.

## 2.3 Sustaining recovery

**Why it matters.** Across Mali, South Sudan and Somalia, 22–63% of children discharged as recovered
relapsed within six months. Depending on the setting, they were 1.2–6.2 times as likely to become acutely
malnourished as matched peers with no episode in the previous year (King et al. 2025). A child who
relapses to severe wasting costs 1.7–2.2 times as much to treat in total as a child who recovers once — an
additional US$64–215 per child for retreatment across the three settings (2022 US$). Because a minority of
children relapse to severe wasting, however, eliminating relapse would save an average of only US$2–45 per
admitted child (Puett et al. 2026).

This is the least-evidenced stage. What predicts relapse is clearer than what prevents it.

| Driver | Lever | Evidence | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Incomplete recovery at discharge | Evaluate discharge criteria and follow-up | King et al. 2025; Kangas et al. 2023; Stobaugh et al. 2017; Guesdon et al. 2026 | Consistent prognostic associations, including a pooled analysis of 21 studies; no randomised test of stricter criteria |
| Food insecurity after discharge | Cash transfers alongside treatment | Grellety et al. 2017; WHO 2023 | One trial; WHO conditional recommendation |
| Relapse risk after discharge | Caregivers screen with MUAC at home | Bountogo et al. 2026 | Small feasibility RCT; primary result inconclusive |
| Illness after discharge | — | Kangas et al. 2023; King et al. 2025 | No intervention reviewed here has an established effect |

**Assess readiness for discharge.** Several studies associate better anthropometric status at discharge
with lower subsequent relapse. These findings identify risk; they do not establish the effect of
prolonging treatment to reach a higher threshold.

- Across Mali, South Sudan and Somalia, better anthropometric status at discharge was associated with
  lower relapse, and few other characteristics measured at discharge predicted it (King et al. 2025).
- Among 420 children treated under a simplified protocol in Mali, each additional millimetre of MUAC at
  discharge was associated with a 23% lower hazard of relapse (aHR 0.77, 95% CI 0.69–0.86), and a
  weight-for-age z-score below −3 at discharge with about three times the hazard (aHR 3.09, 95% CI
  1.69–5.62). The authors conclude that "a higher MUAC cut-off or using an additional anthropometric
  indicator is needed to ensure sustainable recovery" (Kangas et al. 2023).
- In a cluster RCT in Malawi, smaller MUAC and lower weight-for-height at discharge predicted relapse or
  death after moderate acute malnutrition (Stobaugh et al. 2017).
- The largest analysis pooled 21 studies in 13 countries covering 15,394 children discharged as cured.
  Children who still met moderate-wasting criteria at discharge relapsed to severe wasting at 14.2%
  (13.2–15.2) within six months, against 5.2% (4.8–5.6) for those who did not. Children still wasted by
  weight-for-height fared worst — 19.2% against 9.4% for those wasted by MUAC — and a MUAC-only definition
  of severe wasting would have missed more than 60% of relapse cases. The authors conclude that both MUAC
  and weight-for-height should exceed moderate-wasting cut-offs before discharge (Guesdon et al. 2026).

This last finding bears directly on simplified protocols, which discharge on MUAC alone. The Niger trial
found ComPAS non-inferior on its six-month outcome (Daures et al. 2025), but that outcome did not count
moderate wasting by weight-for-height as a failure, so the two findings can both hold. Neither is a
randomised test of discharge criteria. Changing discharge criteria could also lengthen treatment and raise
food use, caregiver burden and defaulting, so the net effect needs evaluation.

**Support households during and after treatment.** In a cluster RCT in the Democratic Republic of the
Congo, households of children treated for uncomplicated severe acute malnutrition received US$40 a month
for six months alongside standard treatment. Relapse fell sharply — adjusted hazard ratios of 0.21
(0.11–0.41) for relapse to moderate and 0.30 (0.16–0.58) for relapse to severe acute malnutrition —
defaulting fell from 6.0% to 1.4%, and the hazard of reaching full recovery rose (aHR 1.35, 95% CI
1.10–1.69) (Grellety et al. 2017). WHO's 2023 guideline conditionally recommends cash transfers alongside
routine care to reduce relapse, on moderate-certainty evidence; it makes no recommendation on cash
transfers for preventing wasting (WHO 2023).

Three caveats apply. This is one trial: a 2023 systematic review of post-discharge interventions
identified no other trial of cash transfers (Bliznashka et al. 2023). The transfers totalled US$240 per
household over six months, before delivery costs — substantial relative to some treatment-cost estimates,
though cross-study costs are not directly comparable. And the trial did not estimate cost-effectiveness, so
ScaleWorks would need to cost the full intervention locally.

**Evaluate caregiver screening after discharge.** A feasibility RCT in Burkina Faso randomised 200
caregiver–child pairs to weekly caregiver MUAC screening or standard follow-up. The primary result — time
to detection of relapse — was inconclusive (HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.38–1.12). Hospitalisation or death was less
frequent in the screening arm (3% vs 14%), but this was a secondary outcome in a small trial (Bountogo et
al. 2026). A full-scale trial is registered (NCT06599580). Earlier detection and fewer relapses are the
intended benefits, not established ones.

**Illness.** Illness after discharge predicts relapse: in Mali, each additional day of illness per month of
follow-up was associated with a 30% higher hazard (aHR 1.30, 95% CI 1.24–1.35) (Kangas et al. 2023; King
et al. 2025). The evidence reviewed here does not identify a post-discharge package that reduces
illness-associated relapse.

**Interventions without established nutritional benefit.** Two approaches did not show consistent
improvements in the nutritional outcomes they assessed.

- A package of lipid-based nutrient supplement, deworming, zinc, a bed net and malaria chemoprophylaxis,
  given after treatment for moderate acute malnutrition in Malawi: 56% of children sustained recovery for a
  year, against 53% of controls, a difference that was not statistically significant (P = 0.38) (Stobaugh
  et al. 2017).
- Psychosocial stimulation for children: three trials in a 2023 systematic review showed no consistent
  nutritional benefit. None measured relapse (Bliznashka et al. 2023).

The Malawi result is a caution against assuming that more services after discharge will reduce relapse.

**Building the evidence.** Relapse is measured inconsistently. A review of 26 studies and programme reports
found relapse ranging from 0% to 37% and concluded that "the lack of a standard definition of relapse
limited comparability" (Stobaugh et al. 2019). A programme designed to scale can help close this gap.

**Implications for ScaleWorks**

- Review discharge practice against national and WHO recommendations. Treat a higher MUAC threshold, or
  adding weight-for-height or weight-for-age, as options to evaluate, with attention to resource use.
- Consider cash transfers where food insecurity is high and funding allows, and cost them from the outset.
- Measure cumulative relapse over six months after discharge, with a prespecified definition that uses
  both MUAC and weight-for-height, and report mortality and loss to follow-up separately. A single
  assessment at month six will miss intervening episodes.

## Summary

| Stage | Recommendation | Strength of evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Reaching treatment | Screen children at every facility contact; decentralise treatment, not only screening; align screening, referral and admission criteria | **Moderate.** One cluster RCT (Gao) with an economic evaluation, plus a single-cluster trial and an observational comparison; WHO and Cochrane rate CHW-delivered treatment as very low certainty |
| Treatment | Use a specified simplified protocol for eligible outpatients, subject to national authorisation; decide the protocol for severely wasted children in light of the Niger findings | **Moderate to strong** for the non-inferiority of ComPAS (two large RCTs); **weaker** for children with MUAC below 115 mm or oedema, where the standard protocol did better; differs from WHO 2023 guidance |
| Sustaining recovery | Review discharge practice, including weight-for-height where feasible; consider cash transfers where food insecurity is high; measure cumulative relapse | **Weakest stage.** Consistent prognostic evidence on discharge status, including a pooled analysis of 21 studies; one trial for cash transfers; feasibility evidence only for caregiver screening |

## Other factors worth exploring

The evidence reviewed here concentrates on delivery, dosing and household support. Less studied is the
caregiver's own wellbeing. A randomised trial in Gombe State, Nigeria, is testing whether a brief
psychological intervention for caregivers with depressive symptoms — WHO's Problem Management Plus,
delivered by community health workers alongside standard CMAM — improves child recovery and growth (Park,
Zhao and Ostrowski, working paper; AEA RCT Registry 13766). Results are not yet public, and the question remains open.
