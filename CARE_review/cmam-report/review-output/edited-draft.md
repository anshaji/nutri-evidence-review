# Treating Child Wasting at Scale

Evidence to inform a ScaleWorks programme for community-based management of acute malnutrition

**Abbreviations:** SAM, severe acute malnutrition; MAM, moderate acute malnutrition; CHW, community health worker; RCT, randomised controlled trial; RUSF, ready-to-use supplementary food; RR, risk ratio; HR, hazard ratio; aHR, adjusted hazard ratio; CI, confidence interval; DALY, disability-adjusted life-year.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Community-based management of acute malnutrition

Community-based management of acute malnutrition (CMAM) combines community identification, outpatient nutritional and medical care, and referral for inpatient treatment when needed. This review focuses principally on children aged 6–59 months. Children with uncomplicated severe wasting who are eligible for outpatient care receive ready-to-use therapeutic food (RUTF) to eat at home and attend regular follow-up visits. Children with medical complications, poor appetite or severe nutritional oedema may require inpatient care. For children aged 6–59 months, severe wasting is identified by a mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) below 115 mm and/or a weight-for-height or weight-for-length z-score below −3. MUAC from 115 mm to less than 125 mm is a criterion for moderate wasting, provided the child does not meet severe criteria. Bilateral pitting oedema is a separate criterion for severe acute malnutrition. Infants younger than six months require a different assessment and care pathway (WHO 2023).

The scale of the problem. The prevalence-based estimate for 2024 was 42.8 million children under five with wasting — 6.6% of the age group — of whom 12.2 million were severely wasted (UNICEF, WHO and World Bank 2025). Severe wasting is associated with a substantially increased risk of death: in a pooled analysis of ten cohorts covering 53,809 children, severely wasted children died at 11.6 times the rate of children with a weight-for-height z-score of at least −1 (Olofin et al. 2013). These observational data establish an association with mortality, not the effect of treatment. The global prevalence figures do not count all episodes occurring during a year.

Treatment works. Ready-to-use therapeutic food probably improves recovery compared with alternative dietary approaches (risk ratio 1.33, 95% CI 1.16–1.54; moderate-certainty evidence) (Schoonees et al. 2019). These trials compare RUTF with alternative dietary approaches; they do not estimate the effect of the full CMAM package compared with no treatment.

Treatment coverage remains a concern. A 2020 global action plan reported that roughly one severely wasted child in three received treatment (FAO et al. 2020). [Editorial query: verify the underlying admissions year and denominator before retaining this historical estimate.] A review of 44 programme coverage assessments in 21 countries, conducted in 2012–2013, found mean coverage of 38.3%. The authors identified possible selection towards better-performing programmes, so coverage outside the sample may have been lower (Rogers et al. 2015). Neither figure should be presented as a current, representative global coverage estimate.

Recovery may not be sustained. In three study settings, 22–63% of children discharged as recovered relapsed to acute malnutrition within six months (King et al. 2025). This range should not be treated as a global estimate or as referring only to relapse to severe wasting.

Coverage, retention and sustained recovery are major implementation priorities. Save the Children’s feedback on the initial review reportedly emphasised adherence and coverage. [Editorial query: add the date and source of this feedback.] Children may be missed before treatment, discontinue treatment, or relapse after discharge. This report is organised around those stages of care.

### 1.2 What ScaleWorks is trying to do

[To be completed with CARE.] ScaleWorks aims to design a CMAM programme that governments can adopt and that can be scaled beyond its first setting. This section needs ScaleWorks’ target geography, population, timeframe and budget envelope.

Three features of the evidence bear directly on that aim.

The gap is largest where burden is high and coverage low. National wasting prevalence was 11.6% in Nigeria in 2021 and 10.9% in Niger in 2022 (World Bank). [Editorial query: attach the dated data extract and confirm both survey estimates.] In Gombe State, northeastern Nigeria, baseline treatment coverage has been estimated at around 1% (GiveWell 2024). [Editorial query: verify the approximately 1% estimate and its geographic and population coverage.]

The delivery and protocol trials highlighted below were conducted mainly in Mali, Niger, Kenya, South Sudan and Burkina Faso, with further evidence from Bangladesh, Burundi, the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Pakistan. Their applicability to other settings requires assessment of health-system capacity, population characteristics, treatment practices and caregiver constraints. This selected evidence base should not be taken to establish that no relevant research exists elsewhere.

National adoption pathways require local assessment. A 2020 study of decision-making in Niger, Nigeria, Somalia and South Sudan found that routine national guidelines generally did not incorporate the proposed combined protocol, although some elements had been accepted for emergency use (Dalglish et al. 2020). This historical finding suggests a possible route for discussion with ministries; it does not establish current permission to use an emergency provision in ScaleWorks’ eventual setting.

## 2. What CARE and ScaleWorks should consider

The interventions below follow the cascade: reaching treatment, treatment itself, and sustaining recovery. For each, the evidence is given with its study design, and the implications for ScaleWorks are drawn out separately.

### 2.1 Getting children into treatment

Why children are missed. When caregivers of untreated children across 44 coverage assessments were asked why their child was not in the programme, five barriers accounted for 84% of answers. Ranked by frequency (Rogers et al. 2015):

1. Not recognising that the child was malnourished
2. Not knowing the programme existed
3. High opportunity costs — travel, lost income, lost agricultural labour
4. The child attending a health facility for another reason, such as malaria, without being screened
5. The child having been screened before and turned away as ineligible

The first two — awareness — made up 55% of all reasons given.

Finding children is not the same as treating them. Two studies illustrate why improved case-finding need not translate into greater treatment coverage.

- In an observational comparison in Burundi, clinic-based systematic screening enrolled 98% of the cases it detected, against 8% for community mass screening: a child identified in a village still has to reach a facility (Odjidja et al. 2022).
- In Mali, a trial that added preventive supplements to community screening raised screening coverage by 40 percentage points but produced no change in treatment coverage; only 7.6% of identified acute malnutrition cases across both arms received appropriate treatment in the month before the endline survey. The authors identified inappropriate provision of preventive supplements to children with acute malnutrition as a possible referral problem; the longitudinal analysis found treatment enrolment 10 percentage points lower (Huybregts et al. 2019).

Decentralising treatment to community health workers. Three studies evaluate bringing treatment, as well as screening, closer to communities.

| Study | Design | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kita, Mali (Alvarez Morán et al. 2018) | Two-cluster comparison, one cluster per arm: CHWs and facilities versus facilities only; analysed as non-randomised in the 2023 Cochrane review | Coverage rose from 43.9% to 86.7%, against 41.6% in the control area; cure 94.2% vs 88.6%; defaulting 4.5% vs 10.8% (RR 0.42, 95% CI 0.25–0.71); no statistically significant difference in mortality detected |
| Gao, Mali (López-Ejeda et al. 2024) | Three-arm cluster RCT, 2,038 children | Recovery 76.3% (facilities), 81.8% (+ CHWs, standard protocol), 92.9% (+ CHWs, simplified protocol). Severe cases: 71.4%, 71.5%, 87.6% |
| Sindh, Pakistan (Rogers et al. 2019) | Cluster RCT: lady health workers alongside facilities vs facilities only | US$291 vs US$301 per child treated; US$382 vs US$363 per child recovered; recovery 76.0% vs 83.0%. The authors report “considerable uncertainty” over which is more cost-effective |

In Gao, severe-case recovery was similar in the facility-only arm and the arm adding community health workers under the standard protocol. Recovery was higher in the arm combining community health workers with a simplified protocol: an unadjusted difference of 16.1 percentage points compared with community health workers using the standard protocol. This supports evaluating the combined approach, but the study did not include a facility-only simplified arm and therefore does not establish that both changes must always be introduced together.

Costs in the Gao setting. An economic evaluation of the Gao trial (Cichon et al. 2025):

| Metric | Facilities only | + CHWs, standard | + CHWs, simplified |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cost per child treated | US$272 | US$179 | US$210 |
| Cost per child cured | US$356 | US$219 | US$226 |
| Cost per DALY averted | US$173 | US$60 | US$53 |

Total programme cost was similar across the three arms. In this evaluation, treating more children spread substantial coordination and supervision costs across a larger caseload. These combined activities accounted for 42–44% of total costs. Local staffing, transport, supervision and caseload assumptions should determine ScaleWorks’ budget; these findings do not establish a universal cost structure or guarantee savings at scale.

A related delivery model. Taimaka provides outpatient treatment through government primary health facilities in Gombe State, Nigeria, using community health extension workers as triage officers, app-guided assessment, MUAC-based admission and nurse supervision. Children requiring inpatient care are referred. This staffing model is related to, but differs from, treatment at additional community sites in Gao. GiveWell’s 2024/2025 assessment modelled a cost of approximately US$105 per child treated and cost-effectiveness of roughly nine times its cash-transfer benchmark. These are model estimates, not trial results. GiveWell reported Taimaka’s 2024 recovery figure as 92.9% after excluding non-response cases; it is self-reported and should not be compared directly with recovery percentages using other denominators. GiveWell also raised uncertainties about effects on other health services, quality of care and scaling (GiveWell 2024/2025).

Guideline position. WHO’s 2023 guideline states that community health workers can assess, classify and manage or refer children with wasting, given adequate training and supervision — a conditional recommendation based on very-low-certainty evidence (WHO 2023).

#### Implications for ScaleWorks

- Screen every child who attends a health facility, whatever the reason for the visit.
- Assess opportunities to decentralise treatment as well as screening; ensure that each screening point has a functioning referral or treatment pathway.
- Align screening, referral and admission criteria with the nationally authorised protocol, including pathways for children identified through oedema or weight-for-height/length criteria. Monitor why referred children are not enrolled.
- Report case-finding and treatment coverage as separate indicators.
- Budget adequately for training, coordination, supervision and referral. Evaluate whether combining decentralisation with protocol simplification is appropriate in the selected setting.

### 2.2 Treating children: simplified or standard protocol

What a simplified protocol changes. Simplified approaches change different elements of care. Combined protocols may treat severe and moderate wasting within one programme using one product, but their admission, discharge and dosing rules differ. ComPAS uses fixed sachet quantities by MUAC category; OptiMA adjusts doses using MUAC and body weight. Reduced-dose trials such as MANGO do not test every component of a combined programme. Under the ComPAS protocol, children with MUAC below 115 mm or oedema receive two sachets a day (about 1,000 kcal); children with MUAC from 115 mm to less than 125 mm receive one (about 500 kcal); the dose steps down once MUAC reaches 115 mm without oedema on two consecutive visits; and children are discharged at 125 mm or above, without oedema, on two consecutive visits (Bailey et al. 2020).

The evidence

| Study | Design | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ComPAS (Bailey et al. 2020) | Cluster RCT, 4,110 children, Kenya and South Sudan | Per-protocol recovery: 76.3% vs 73.5% — non-inferior; reported per-protocol mortality: 1.8% in both arms. Unadjusted severe-case defaulting: 33.8% vs 44.3%. Among recovered children admitted with SAM, 122 vs 193 sachets of ready-to-use food; the standard arm included RUTF and RUSF. Cost per child recovered: US$918 vs US$1,041. The trial did not establish a general difference in defaulting across its secondary analyses. |
| OptiMA–ComPAS (Daures et al. 2025) | Individually randomised, Niger: 1,732 children in the primary analysis; 1,140 in the severe-case analysis, with additional recruitment for that analysis | Primary outcome at six months after enrolment: alive, meeting the study’s nutritional criteria and without an additional episode. ComPAS was non-inferior in intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses; OptiMA in intention-to-treat only. In the severe-case analysis, ComPAS and OptiMA used approximately 50% and 32% less RUTF, respectively. No statistically significant mortality difference was detected. |
| MANGO (Kangas et al. 2019) | RCT, Burkina Faso | Reduced RUTF dose non-inferior on weight gain, with a small negative effect on height gain (0.2 mm/week), more pronounced among children younger than 12 months |

Using the ComPAS unit-cost estimates, a fixed budget could fund approximately 13% more recoveries if costs remained constant as the programme expanded (US$1,041/US$918 − 1). This is an illustrative calculation, not an observed scale-up effect. The Niger endpoint was assessed six months after enrolment; it is not equivalent to six months of follow-up after discharge.

Where the standard protocol did better. Among children in the Niger severe-case analysis, the standard protocol achieved higher nutritional recovery in both intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses. The authors attributed much of the difference to attendance; missed visits were more frequent in the simplified arms. This interpretation does not establish that enhanced follow-up would eliminate the difference, because that remedy was not tested (Daures et al. 2025).

What simplification saves depends on the setting. In Gao, the simplified community-inclusive arm cost more per child treated than the standard community-inclusive arm (US$210 vs US$179), while costs per child recovered were similar (US$226 vs US$219). RUTF costs for severe cases were US$5.70 lower per child under the simplified protocol. Food accounted for a relatively small share of total programme costs in this evaluation. ScaleWorks should therefore model the entire programme rather than assume that lower food use translates proportionately into lower overall costs (Cichon et al. 2025).

Formulation matters for relapse. The review found little or no difference in recovery between standard and alternative RUTF formulations (RR 1.03, 95% CI 0.99–1.08; high-certainty evidence), but standard RUTF reduces relapse compared with alternative formulations (RR 0.84, 95% CI 0.72–0.98; high-certainty evidence) (Schoonees et al. 2019). This result applies to the formulations and relapse outcomes assessed in the review; it should not be generalised to every lower-cost formulation.

Guideline position. WHO’s 2023 guideline retains RUTF dosing indexed to body weight — 150–185 kcal/kg/day, with an option to step down to 100–130 kcal/kg/day once severe wasting and nutritional oedema have resolved — and discharge on both weight-for-height and MUAC (WHO 2023). The fixed-dose, MUAC-only approach described here therefore differs from WHO’s 2023 recommendations on dosing and discharge.

#### Implications for ScaleWorks

- Consider a specified simplified protocol for eligible outpatients, subject to national authorisation and local assessment of effectiveness, acceptability and feasibility.
- Specify the protocol for children with uncomplicated severe wasting separately from that for moderate wasting. Take the Niger recovery findings into account; evaluate attendance support without assuming that it will remove the difference.
- Assess simplification and decentralisation both individually and as a combined delivery option.
- Discuss current national guidance, authorisation and evaluation requirements with ministries at the outset.

### 2.3 Sustaining recovery

Why it matters. Across Mali, South Sudan and Somalia, 22–63% of children discharged as recovered relapsed within six months, and they were three times as likely to become acutely malnourished as their matched community peers with no episode of acute malnutrition in the preceding year (King et al. 2025). Relapse also increases treatment costs (Puett et al. 2026). [Editorial query: confirm whether the reported 1.7–2.2 ratio compares total initial treatment plus retreatment with initial treatment alone; do not label it the cost of retreatment alone without checking the tables. Verify the assumptions and denominator for the draft’s US$2–45 potential saving per admitted child.]

This is the least-evidenced stage of the cascade. What predicts relapse is clearer than what prevents it.

| Driver | Lever | Evidence | Strength |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Incomplete recovery at discharge | Evaluate discharge criteria and follow-up needs | King et al. 2025; Kangas et al. 2023; Stobaugh et al. 2017 | Consistent prognostic associations; not a randomised test of stricter discharge criteria |
| Food insecurity after discharge | Cash transfers alongside treatment | Grellety et al. 2017; WHO 2023 | One trial; WHO conditional recommendation |
| Risk of relapse after discharge | Caregivers screen with MUAC at home | Bountogo et al. 2026 | Small published feasibility RCT; primary result inconclusive |
| Illness after discharge | — | Kangas et al. 2023; King et al. 2025 | No established relapse-prevention effect from the interventions reviewed here |

Assess readiness for discharge. Several studies associate better anthropometric status at discharge with lower subsequent relapse. These findings identify risk; they do not establish the effect of prolonging treatment to reach a higher threshold.

- Across Mali, South Sudan and Somalia, better anthropometric status at discharge was associated with lower relapse risk, and few other characteristics measured at discharge predicted relapse (King et al. 2025).
- Among 420 children treated under a simplified protocol in Mali, each additional millimetre of MUAC at discharge was associated with a 23% lower hazard of relapse (aHR 0.77, 95% CI 0.69–0.86), and a weight-for-age z-score below −3 at discharge was associated with approximately three times the hazard (aHR 3.09, 95% CI 1.69–5.62). The authors conclude that “a higher MUAC cut-off or using an additional anthropometric indicator is needed to ensure sustainable recovery” (Kangas et al. 2023).
- In a cluster RCT in Malawi, smaller MUAC and lower weight-for-height at discharge predicted relapse or death after moderate acute malnutrition (Stobaugh et al. 2017).

Changing discharge criteria could increase treatment duration, food use, caregiver burden and defaulting. The net effect should be evaluated. A 2026 pooled analysis of 21 studies involving 15,394 children also found higher subsequent SAM relapse among children who still met MAM criteria at discharge, supporting attention to both MUAC and weight-for-height status (Guesdon et al. 2026). This remains observational evidence about discharge status. The Niger result provides evidence for one specific protocol and enrolment-based endpoint; it does not establish that every simplified protocol preserves six-month post-discharge recovery.

Support households during and after treatment. In a cluster RCT in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, households of children treated for uncomplicated severe acute malnutrition received US$40 a month for six months alongside standard treatment. Relapse fell sharply — adjusted hazard ratios of 0.21 (0.11–0.41) for relapse to moderate and 0.30 (0.16–0.58) for relapse to severe acute malnutrition — defaulting fell from 6.0% to 1.4%, and the hazard of reaching full recovery rose (aHR 1.35, 95% CI 1.10–1.69) (Grellety et al. 2017). WHO’s 2023 guideline conditionally recommends cash transfers alongside routine care to reduce relapse, on moderate-certainty evidence. It makes no recommendation on cash transfers for preventing wasting (WHO 2023).

Three caveats apply. This is one trial: a 2023 systematic review of post-discharge interventions identified no other trial of cash transfers (Bliznashka et al. 2023). The scheduled transfers totalled US$240 per participating household over six months, before delivery costs. This is substantial relative to some treatment-cost estimates, but cross-study costs are not directly comparable. The trial did not establish the cost-effectiveness of adding cash transfers; ScaleWorks would need to estimate the full local cost.

Evaluate caregiver screening after discharge. A published feasibility RCT in Burkina Faso enrolled 200 caregiver–child pairs. Its primary time-to-relapse-detection result was inconclusive (HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.38–1.12). Hospitalisation or death occurred less often in the screening arm, but this was a secondary result in a small trial and requires confirmation (Bountogo et al. 2026). Caregiver MUAC screening is intended to support detection and referral; earlier detection and relapse prevention should not be described as established benefits. [Editorial query: add a registry citation before stating that a larger trial is registered.]

Illness. Illness after discharge predicts relapse: in Mali, each additional day of illness per month of follow-up was associated with a 30% higher hazard of relapse (aHR 1.30, 95% CI 1.24–1.35) (Kangas et al. 2023; King et al. 2025). The evidence reviewed here does not establish which post-discharge package would reduce illness-associated relapse.

Interventions without established nutritional benefit. The following studies did not establish consistent improvements in the nutritional outcomes assessed. Outcomes that were not measured should not be described as unaffected.

- A package of lipid-based nutrient supplement, deworming, zinc, a bed net and malaria chemoprophylaxis, given after treatment for moderate acute malnutrition in Malawi: 56% of children sustained recovery for a year, compared with 53% of controls; the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.38) (Stobaugh et al. 2017).
- Psychosocial stimulation for children: three trials in a 2023 systematic review showed no consistent nutritional benefit, and none measured relapse (Bliznashka et al. 2023).

The Malawi result is a caution against assuming that more services after discharge will reduce relapse.

Building the evidence. Relapse is measured inconsistently. A review of 26 studies and programme reports found relapse ranging from 0% to 37% and concluded that “the lack of a standard definition of relapse limited comparability” (Stobaugh et al. 2019). A programme designed to scale can help close this gap by building in six-month post-discharge follow-up with a fixed definition and, where feasible, by comparing discharge thresholds within its rollout.

#### Implications for ScaleWorks

- Review discharge practice against national and WHO recommendations. Treat higher MUAC thresholds or additional weight-for-age criteria as options for evaluation, with attention to resource use and sustained recovery.
- Consider cash transfers where food insecurity is high and funding allows, and cost them from the outset.
- Measure cumulative relapse during six months after discharge using a prespecified definition, and report mortality and loss to follow-up separately. A single assessment at month six will miss intervening episodes.

## Summary

| Stage | Recommendation | Strength of evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reaching treatment | Assess decentralised treatment; screen children at facility contacts; align screening, referral and admission pathways | Evidence varies by intervention and outcome; includes randomised and observational studies and economic evaluations |
| Treatment | Choose a specified protocol for eligible outpatients; distinguish severe and moderate groups; evaluate attendance support | Randomised evidence supports some protocols and outcomes; severe-case findings differ by setting, and some approaches differ from WHO guidance |
| Sustaining recovery | Review discharge practice; evaluate additional criteria and household support; track sustained recovery | Prognostic evidence for discharge status; one principal cash-transfer trial in the cited review; limited evidence for other post-discharge interventions |
