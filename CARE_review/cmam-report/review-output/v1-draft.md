# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Community-based management of acute malnutrition

Community-based management of acute malnutrition (CMAM) treats children with wasting — low weight for
their height — as outpatients. Children receive ready-to-use therapeutic food (RUTF) to eat at home and
return for weekly or fortnightly checks; those with medical complications are first stabilised as
inpatients. Wasting is classified by mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC): below 115 mm is severe acute
malnutrition, and 115–124 mm is moderate. Nutritional oedema classifies a child as severely malnourished
whatever the MUAC reading.

**The scale of the problem.** In 2024, 42.8 million children under five were wasted — 6.6% of the age
group — of whom 12.2 million were severely wasted (UNICEF, WHO and World Bank 2025). Severe wasting is
lethal: in a pooled analysis of ten cohorts covering 53,809 children, severely wasted children died at
11.6 times the rate of children with normal weight-for-height (Olofin et al. 2013).

**Treatment works.** Ready-to-use therapeutic food probably improves recovery compared with alternative
dietary approaches (risk ratio 1.33, 95% CI 1.16–1.54; moderate certainty) (Schoonees et al. 2019). CMAM
has never been trialled against no treatment, and it would now be unethical to do so, so its measured
benefit understates its true one.

**Most children never receive it.** Roughly one severely wasted child in three receives treatment, on
2019 admissions data (FAO et al. 2020). The largest multi-country survey of programme coverage — 44
assessments across 21 countries — found mean coverage of 38.3%, and its authors noted that
better-performing programmes were likelier to be assessed, so the true figure is lower (Rogers et al.
2015).

**Recovery often does not last.** Between 22% and 63% of children discharged as recovered relapse to
acute malnutrition within six months, depending on setting (King et al. 2025).

The binding constraint is therefore not efficacy. Save the Children's feedback on the initial review
framed it as adherence and coverage, and the evidence bears that out: children are lost before they reach
treatment, during it, and after discharge. This report is organised around that cascade.

## 1.2 What ScaleWorks is trying to do

> **[To be completed with CARE.]** ScaleWorks aims to design a CMAM programme that governments can adopt
> and that can be scaled beyond its first setting. This section needs ScaleWorks' target geography,
> population, time frame and budget envelope.

Three features of the evidence bear directly on that aim.

**The gap is largest where burden is high and coverage low.** National wasting prevalence was 11.6% in
Nigeria in 2021 and 10.9% in Niger in 2022 (World Bank). In Gombe State, northeastern Nigeria, baseline
treatment coverage has been estimated at around 1% (GiveWell 2024).

**The evidence base is regional.** The trials that support the interventions below were conducted mainly
in Mali, Niger, Kenya, South Sudan and Burkina Faso, with further studies in Bangladesh, Burundi, the
Democratic Republic of the Congo and Pakistan. Applying them elsewhere — including India, which has more
wasted children than any other country — rests on judgement about transferability rather than on direct
evidence.

**A route to adoption exists.** A 2020 study of national decision-making in Niger, Nigeria, Somalia and
South Sudan found that national guidelines "generally did not reflect the innovations proposed in the
Combined Protocol", but that aspects had been accepted for use in emergencies (Dalglish et al. 2020). A
programme can begin under that emergency provision and generate national data to support routine
adoption.

# 2. What CARE and ScaleWorks should consider

The interventions below follow the cascade: reaching treatment, treatment itself, and sustaining
recovery. For each, the evidence is given with its study design, and the implications for ScaleWorks are
drawn out separately.

## 2.1 Getting children into treatment

**Why children are missed.** When caregivers of untreated children across 44 coverage assessments were
asked why their child was not in the programme, five barriers accounted for 84% of answers. Ranked by
frequency (Rogers et al. 2015):

1. Not recognising that the child was malnourished
2. Not knowing the programme existed
3. High opportunity costs — travel, lost income, lost agricultural labour
4. The child attending a health facility for another reason, such as malaria, without being screened
5. The child having been screened before and turned away as ineligible

The first two — awareness — made up 55% of all reasons given.

**Finding children is not the same as treating them.** Two trials show that case-finding and treatment
coverage can move independently.

- In Burundi, clinic-based systematic screening enrolled 98% of the cases it detected, against 8% for
  community mass screening: a child identified in a village still has to reach a facility (Odjidja et al.
  2022).
- In Mali, a trial that added preventive supplements to community screening raised screening coverage by
  40 percentage points but produced no change in treatment coverage; only 7.6% of acutely malnourished
  children received appropriate treatment. Volunteers gave identified children the supplements instead of
  referring them, and enrolment in treatment fell by 10 percentage points (Huybregts et al. 2019).

**Decentralising treatment to community health workers.** Three trials test moving treatment itself —
not only screening — into the community.

| | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Kita, Mali (Alvarez Morán et al. 2018) | Randomised: CHWs and facilities vs facilities only | Coverage rose from 43.9% to **86.7%**, against 41.6% in the control area; cure 94.2% vs 88.6%; defaulting **4.5% vs 10.8%** (RR 0.42, 0.25–0.71); no difference in mortality |
| Gao, Mali (López-Ejeda et al. 2024) | Three-arm cluster RCT, 2,038 children | Recovery 76.3% (facilities), 81.8% (+ CHWs, standard protocol), **92.9%** (+ CHWs, simplified protocol). Severe cases: 71.4%, 71.5%, **87.6%** |
| Sindh, Pakistan (Rogers et al. 2019) | Cluster RCT: lady health workers alongside facilities vs facilities only | $291 vs $301 per child treated; $382 vs $363 per child recovered; recovery 76.0% vs 83.0%. The authors report "considerable uncertainty" over which is more cost-effective |

The Gao result carries the section's central point. Adding community health workers to the standard
protocol made no difference to severe-case recovery; community health workers using a simplified
protocol raised it by 16 percentage points. **Decentralisation and simplification work together.**

**Why decentralisation reduces cost.** An economic evaluation of the Gao trial (Cichon et al. 2025):

| | Facilities only | + CHWs, standard | + CHWs, simplified |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost per child treated | $272 | $179 | $210 |
| Cost per child cured | $356 | $219 | $226 |
| Cost per DALY averted | $173 | $60 | $53 |

Total programme cost was similar across the three arms. The fall in cost per child came from treating
more children with the same fixed costs: coordination and supervision made up 42–44% of total cost and
did not scale with caseload. **Decentralisation lowers cost per child only if it raises the number
treated.**

**A programme already operating this model.** Taimaka delivers community-based treatment in Gombe State,
Nigeria, through government primary health facilities — using community health extension workers as
triage officers, app-guided diagnosis and treatment, MUAC-based admission, and nurse supervision of
complicated cases. GiveWell estimates its cost at about $105 per child treated and its cost-effectiveness
at roughly nine times that of unconditional cash transfers (GiveWell 2024). Taimaka reports 92.9%
recovery in 2024; the figure is self-reported. GiveWell's stated reservations include the programme's
effects on other health services and on quality of care.

**Guideline position.** WHO's 2023 guideline states that community health workers can assess, classify
and manage or refer children with wasting, given adequate training and supervision — a conditional
recommendation on very low certainty evidence (WHO 2023).

**Implications for ScaleWorks**

- Screen every child who attends a health facility, whatever the reason for the visit.
- Decentralise treatment, not only screening; every screening point should connect to a treatment point.
- Use MUAC for both screening and admission, so that referred children are not turned away.
- Report case-finding and treatment coverage as separate indicators.
- Budget supervision as the largest cost line, and introduce decentralisation together with a simplified
  protocol.

## 2.2 Treating children: simplified or standard protocol

**What a simplified protocol changes.** Simplified protocols treat severe and moderate wasting in one
programme with one product, admit and discharge on MUAC, and set the RUTF dose by MUAC band rather than by
body weight. Under the ComPAS protocol, children with MUAC below 115 mm or oedema receive two sachets a
day (about 1,000 kcal); children at 115–124 mm receive one (about 500 kcal); the dose steps down once MUAC
reaches 115 mm without oedema on two consecutive visits; and children are discharged at 125 mm or above,
without oedema, on two consecutive visits (Bailey et al. 2020).

**The evidence**

| | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| ComPAS (Bailey et al. 2020) | Cluster RCT, 4,110 children, Kenya and South Sudan | Recovery **76.3% vs 73.5%** — non-inferior; mortality 1.8% in both arms; defaulting among severe cases **33.8% vs 44.3%**; 122 vs 193 sachets per child recovered; **$918 vs $1,041** per child recovered |
| OptiMA–ComPAS (Daures et al. 2025) | Individually randomised, 1,732 children, Niger | Primary outcome: alive, recovered and no further episode over six months. ComPAS (50% less RUTF) non-inferior on intention-to-treat and per-protocol analysis; OptiMA (32% less) on intention-to-treat only. No difference in mortality |
| MANGO (Kangas et al. 2019) | RCT, Burkina Faso | Reduced RUTF dose non-inferior on weight gain, with a small negative effect on height gain (0.2 mm/week), more pronounced under 12 months |

The ComPAS cost result implies about 13% more children recovered for a fixed budget. The Niger primary
outcome includes relapse over six months, so its recovery figures are not comparable with those of
ComPAS.

**Where the standard protocol did better.** Among 1,140 children in the Niger trial with MUAC below 115 mm
or oedema, the standard protocol achieved higher recovery on both analyses. The authors attribute this
"primarily" to "better attendance rather than differences in mortality or growth"; both simplified arms
had 20% more missed visits (Daures et al. 2025).

**What simplification saves.** Less than the reduction in therapeutic food suggests. In the Gao
evaluation, the same providers with and without the simplified protocol showed little difference in cost
per child, and the saving on therapeutic food for severe cases was $5.70 per child; therapeutic food made
up only about 8–12% of total programme cost in that evaluation (Cichon et al. 2025). Simplification
contributes mainly to recovery and retention — particularly where treatment is delivered by community
health workers — rather than to cost.

**Formulation matters for relapse.** Different RUTF formulations recover children equally well (RR 1.03,
0.99–1.08; high certainty), but standard RUTF reduces relapse compared with alternative formulations (RR
0.84, 0.72–0.98; high certainty) (Schoonees et al. 2019). Cheaper formulations are not cost-free.

**Guideline position.** WHO's 2023 guideline retains RUTF dosing indexed to body weight — 150–185
kcal/kg/day, with a step-down to 100–130 kcal/kg/day once a child is no longer severely wasted — and
discharge on both weight-for-height and MUAC (WHO 2023). A simplified protocol therefore operates ahead of
WHO guidance on dosing and discharge.

**Implications for ScaleWorks**

- Use a simplified protocol for the uncomplicated caseload.
- For children with MUAC below 115 mm or oedema, either retain the standard protocol or pair
  simplification with reinforced follow-up, since the gap in Niger came from attendance.
- Introduce simplification together with decentralisation.
- Raise the guideline position with ministries at the outset rather than after adoption.

## 2.3 Sustaining recovery

**Why it matters.** Across Mali, South Sudan and Somalia, 22–63% of children discharged as recovered
relapsed within six months, and they were three times as likely to become acutely malnourished as their
never-malnourished neighbours (King et al. 2025). Retreating a child who relapses to severe wasting costs
1.7–2.2 times the initial recovery (Puett et al. 2026). Averting relapse would nonetheless save only
$2–45 per admitted child at programme level, because a minority of children relapse to severe wasting.

This is the least-evidenced stage of the cascade. What predicts relapse is clearer than what prevents it.

| Driver | Lever | Evidence | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Incomplete recovery at discharge | Discharge on stricter criteria | King et al. 2025; Kangas et al. 2023; Stobaugh et al. 2017 | Convergent — three studies |
| Food insecurity after discharge | Cash transfers alongside treatment | Grellety et al. 2017; WHO 2023 | One trial; WHO conditional recommendation |
| Relapse occurring regardless | Caregivers screen with MUAC at home | Bountogo et al. 2026 | Feasibility trial only |
| Illness after discharge | — | Kangas et al. 2023; King et al. 2025 | No proven intervention |

**Discharge children in better condition.** This is the strongest evidence in the section. Three studies
in different settings, using different designs, find that children discharged in better anthropometric
condition relapse less.

- Across Mali, South Sudan and Somalia, better anthropometric status at discharge was protective, and few
  other characteristics measured at discharge predicted relapse (King et al. 2025).
- Among 420 children treated under a simplified protocol in Mali, each additional millimetre of MUAC at
  discharge reduced the hazard of relapse by 23% (adjusted hazard ratio 0.77, 0.69–0.86), and a
  weight-for-age z-score below −3 at discharge tripled it (aHR 3.09, 1.69–5.62). The authors conclude that
  "a higher MUAC cut-off or using an additional anthropometric indicator is needed to ensure sustainable
  recovery" (Kangas et al. 2023).
- In a cluster RCT in Malawi, smaller MUAC and lower weight-for-height at discharge predicted relapse or
  death after moderate acute malnutrition (Stobaugh et al. 2017).

Discharge criteria are a protocol setting, so this lever is inexpensive to change. The trade-off is
longer treatment, more therapeutic food, and possibly more defaulting. Simplification itself does not
appear to worsen sustained recovery: the Niger trial's six-month outcome includes relapse, and ComPAS was
non-inferior on it (Daures et al. 2025).

**Support households after discharge.** In a cluster RCT in the Democratic Republic of the Congo,
households of children treated for uncomplicated severe acute malnutrition received US$40 a month for six
months alongside standard treatment. Relapse fell sharply — adjusted hazard ratios of 0.21 (0.11–0.41) for
relapse to moderate and 0.30 (0.16–0.58) for relapse to severe acute malnutrition — defaulting fell from
6.0% to 1.4%, and the hazard of reaching full recovery rose (aHR 1.35, 1.10–1.69) (Grellety et al. 2017).
WHO's 2023 guideline conditionally recommends cash transfers alongside routine care to reduce relapse, on
moderate-certainty evidence. It makes no recommendation on cash transfers for preventing wasting (WHO
2023).

Three caveats apply. This is one trial: a 2023 systematic review of post-discharge interventions
identified no other trial of cash transfers (Bliznashka et al. 2023). The transfer — $240 per child — is
comparable to the cost of treatment itself. And the trial did not cost the intervention, so its
cost-effectiveness is unknown.

**Detect relapse early.** Training caregivers to check their child's MUAC at home after discharge is
being tested. A feasibility RCT of 200 caregiver–child pairs in Burkina Faso has been completed (Bountogo
et al. 2026), and a full-scale trial is registered. The approach does not prevent relapse; its purpose is
to return children to treatment before they deteriorate further.

**Illness.** Illness after discharge predicts relapse: in Mali, each additional day sick per month of
follow-up raised the hazard by 30% (aHR 1.30, 1.24–1.35) (Kangas et al. 2023; King et al. 2025). No
post-discharge intervention has been shown to address it.

**What has not worked.** Two approaches have been tested without effect on relapse or nutritional status.

- A package of lipid-based nutrient supplement, deworming, zinc, a bed net and malaria chemoprophylaxis,
  given after treatment for moderate acute malnutrition in Malawi: 56% of children sustained recovery for
  a year, against 53% of controls — no significant difference (P = 0.38) (Stobaugh et al. 2017).
- Psychosocial stimulation for children: three trials in a 2023 systematic review showed no consistent
  nutritional benefit, and none measured relapse (Bliznashka et al. 2023).

The Malawi result is a caution against assuming that more services after discharge will reduce relapse.

**Building the evidence.** Relapse is measured inconsistently. A review of 26 studies and programme
reports found relapse ranging from 0% to 37% and concluded that "the lack of a standard definition of
relapse limited comparability" (Stobaugh et al. 2019). A programme designed to scale can help close this
gap by building in six-month post-discharge follow-up with a fixed definition and, where feasible, by
comparing discharge thresholds within its rollout.

**Implications for ScaleWorks**

- Review discharge criteria: a higher MUAC threshold, or adding weight-for-age, is the best-supported
  lever.
- Consider cash transfers where food insecurity is high and funding allows, and cost them from the
  outset.
- Measure relapse at six months, using a fixed definition.

## Summary

| Stage | Recommendation | Strength of evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Reaching treatment | Decentralise treatment to community health workers; screen all children attending facilities; use MUAC for both screening and admission | Strong — several randomised trials, with costing |
| Treatment | Simplified protocol for the uncomplicated caseload; standard protocol or reinforced follow-up for MUAC below 115 mm or oedema | Strong — two large randomised trials; ahead of WHO guidance |
| Sustaining recovery | Tighten discharge criteria; add cash transfers where feasible; measure relapse | Convergent for discharge criteria; one trial for cash |

## Other factors worth exploring

The evidence reviewed here concentrates on delivery, dosing and household income. Less studied is the
caregiver's own wellbeing. A randomised trial in Gombe State, Nigeria, is testing whether a brief
psychological intervention for caregivers with depressive symptoms — WHO's Problem Management Plus,
delivered by community health workers alongside standard CMAM — improves child recovery and growth (Park,
Zhao and Ostrowski, working paper). Results are not yet public, and the question remains open.
