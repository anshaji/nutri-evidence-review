Each section sets out the problem and the mechanism that makes it one; the intervention and why
it works; the caveats that bound the claim; the implications for government adoption; and the
geographies with the strongest case. Caveats are stated explicitly because each marks a point at
which a defensible summary can drift past what the evidence supports.

There are four sections rather than three. Breastfeeding support is treated separately, because
the evidence for it is stronger than a first reading suggests.

---

# 1. Treating child wasting (CMAM)

## The problem

Approximately **one severely wasted child in three receives treatment** (Global Action Plan on
Child Wasting, on 2019 admissions data); **two in three go untreated**.

The consequence is not deferred. Severe wasting carries roughly a **twelvefold mortality hazard**
relative to normal weight-for-height. An effective treatment exists, and most eligible children
never reach it.

Nor does treatment close the episode. **Between 22% and 63%** of children discharged as recovered
relapse within six months, depending on setting. Better anthropometric status at discharge is
protective, but beyond that few discharge-time characteristics predict relapse: what does is
household food insecurity and morbidity **afterwards** — determinants that sit outside the
treatment episode. The implication is discharge criteria *and* post-discharge support, not one
in place of the other.

## The solution

**Simplified protocols for the uncomplicated caseload.** These use **32–50% less** ready-to-use
therapeutic food while holding recovery. In a 2025 three-arm trial in Niger, the combined
SAM/MAM protocol (ComPAS, 50% reduction) met non-inferiority on both intention-to-treat and
per-protocol analysis; the tapered-dose protocol (OptiMA, 32% reduction) met it on
intention-to-treat only.

*Mechanism.* Therapeutic food is the largest single line item — 43% of institutional cost in the
Ethiopian community programme where it was measured — and the component of the standard protocol
most generous relative to the requirement of an uncomplicated case. The ComPAS trial measured the
result directly: **US$918 per child recovered under the combined protocol against US$1,041 under
the standard protocol**, using 122 rather than 193 sachets. That is a **12% saving, or roughly
13% more children recovered for the same budget**.

**Cash transfers alongside treatment.** A cluster-randomised trial in the Democratic Republic of
Congo added US$40 per month for six months to standard treatment. Relapse fell by **70–80%**
(adjusted hazard ratios 0.21 to moderate and 0.30 to severe acute malnutrition) and recovery rose
**35%**.

The 35% refers to the hazard of reaching full recovery, not to the proportion recovered.
**WHO's 2023 guideline conditionally recommends cash transfers in addition to routine care to
reduce relapse**, on moderate-certainty evidence.

*Mechanism.* The transfer addresses the household food insecurity that drives relapse. Transfer
size may be the active ingredient — a pooled review found no effect from standard unconditional
transfers and a significant effect from doubled transfers — but this is contested, and at least
one trial of a doubled transfer found no nutritional gain.

**Screening without a treatment pathway does not work.** In Mali, a trial raised screening
coverage by **40 percentage points** with no change in treatment coverage. In Burundi, community
mass screening enrolled **8%** of detected cases against **98%** for clinic-based systematic
screening.

*Mechanism, Mali.* Community volunteers distributed preventive supplements to the children they
identified rather than referring them, and enrolment in treatment **fell** by 10 percentage
points. *Mechanism, Burundi.* Community detection introduces a referral step that clinic
detection does not, since a child identified at a facility is already at the point of treatment.
In both cases case-finding and treatment coverage are distinct measures, and performance on one
is uninformative about the other.

## Caveats

- Simplified protocols are not indicated across the caseload. Among children with mid-upper arm
  circumference below 115 mm or nutritional oedema, the **standard protocol achieved higher
  recovery**, driven by attendance rather than by mortality or growth.
- Cash transfers add materially to the cost per child — the DRC transfer was US$240 against a
  treatment cost of roughly US$200–300. Generalisable cost-effectiveness is not established; the
  trial itself did not cost the intervention.
- Decentralisation is not inherently cheaper. In Pakistan, community health worker delivery cost
  marginally less per child *treated* ($291 against $301) and more per child *recovered* ($382
  against $363), alongside lower recovery (76% against 83%). The authors tested neither
  difference and report "considerable uncertainty in which strategy was most cost-effective".

## Opportunities in government adoption

- Extend supply chains **past the health facility to community health workers**, so the commodity
  reaches the point at which treatment now occurs.
- Retrain frontline workers on the simplified protocol, retaining the standard protocol for
  severe cases.
- **Report case-finding and treatment coverage as separate indicators.** A single coverage target
  conceals the failure point.
- Where budgets permit, add post-discharge cash transfers.

*A flag for policy makers:* simplified protocols operate **ahead of current WHO guidance**, which
retains weight-indexed dosing. That position is defensible and fundable, but better raised early
than discovered late.

## Countries to focus

**India** (18.7% wasting) is the largest single concentration globally and has a functioning
routine system to build on. **Yemen, Sudan, Nigeria, Niger, Bangladesh and Burkina Faso** carry
the second concentration, where caseloads surge episodically and simplified protocols have most
to offer. Sudan's figure dates from 2014, before the current conflict, and understates the
current position.

# 2. Kangaroo mother care

## The problem

**One infant in seven worldwide is born with low birthweight** — 19.8 million a year, and one in
four in South Asia. On a broader definition — preterm or small for gestational age, roughly one
birth in four — **55% of neonatal deaths** are attributable to infants born too small: about
**1.3 million of the 2.4 million** neonatal deaths in 2020.

*Mechanism.* Low-birthweight and preterm infants thermoregulate poorly, feed inefficiently and
have impaired immune defence. The conventional response is incubator care, which is scarce,
costly and frequently unavailable where these births occur.

## The solution

**Kangaroo mother care — continuous skin-to-skin contact on the mother's chest, 8 to 24 hours a
day, initiated as soon as possible after birth.** It **reduces mortality by 32%** among preterm
and low-birthweight infants, at high certainty.

*Mechanism.* Skin-to-skin contact substitutes for the incubator: it stabilises the infant's
temperature, facilitates breastfeeding and reduces infection. No commodity, device or supply
chain is required.

The 32% is kangaroo care against conventional care, irrespective of timing. Starting within 24
hours adds a further reduction against a later start (RR 0.77, 0.66–0.91, high certainty).

**What changed in 2022.** Kangaroo care was historically initiated only after clinical
stabilisation — in all but one trial in the pooled evidence base, infants were stabilised before
enrolment. A WHO multi-country trial of immediate initiation led WHO to revise its guidance in
2022 to **start as soon as possible after birth**, a **strong recommendation on high-certainty
evidence** and the strongest normative backing of anything in this review. A subsequent trial in
Uganda (OMWaNA, 2,221 neonates) found **no reduction in early neonatal mortality** from starting
before stabilisation, so the case for immediate initiation is strong but not uncontested.

**Cost.** There is no commodity, but there is a set-up cost. The Uganda trial put ward readiness
at **$31,000–$96,000 per hospital in financial cost, or $45,000–$114,000 in economic cost**, and
found that once operating, kangaroo care **reduced** the cost of newborn care to both providers
and households.

## Caveats

- The effect is measured in **preterm and low-birthweight infants** — approximately one birth in
  seven. Applied to newborns generally, it overstates the eligible population roughly sevenfold.
- Cost per infant is minimal, but the intervention requires mother and infant to remain together
  under clinical monitoring. That is a ward reconfiguration, and it is where the expenditure sits.

## Opportunities in government adoption

- Write **immediate** initiation into facility delivery protocols, replacing initiation after
  stabilisation.
- Fund the ward changes that allow mothers to remain with their infants: space, beds, staffing.
- Invest in staff training and supervision, since uptake depends on personnel rather than
  equipment.

## Countries to focus

**South Asia**, decisively. Low birthweight is highest there (24.8%), facility delivery is
rising, and the intervention reaches only facility births. The IGAD countries of the Horn of
Africa sit at 14.6%, close to the global average, with lower facility delivery coverage.

# 3. Antenatal multiple micronutrient supplements (MMS)

## The problem

In most low- and middle-income countries, antenatal care provides **iron and folic acid — two
nutrients**. Women in these settings are typically deficient in considerably more.

*Mechanism.* Micronutrient shortfall in pregnancy restricts fetal growth. The result is low
birthweight, which raises neonatal mortality risk and carries durable consequences for health
and earnings.

## The solution

**Substitute a multiple micronutrient supplement for iron-folic acid — 15 nutrients rather than
2, through the same tablet, visit and channel.** It reduces **low birthweight by 12%** and
**small-for-gestational-age births by 8%**.

*Mechanism.* It corrects more of the underlying deficiency. Effects are largest in **anaemic and
underweight women**, consistent with a nutrient-shortfall mechanism: benefit scales with baseline
deficiency.

**Adoption is unusually straightforward.** The commodity substitutes into an existing channel. No
new service, visit or cadre is required, and production cost is approximately half a US cent
higher per tablet.

**Cost.** Procurement runs roughly **$2.00 per pregnancy for iron-folic acid against $3.42 for
MMS**. A fixed budget therefore covers fewer women, so the appropriate measure is cost per case
averted: on our arithmetic, approximately **$48 per low-birthweight birth averted** in South Asia
on the commodity gap alone. The published cost-effectiveness estimates are $21–42 per DALY
averted across Bangladesh, India and Pakistan.

## Caveats

- There is **no evidence that MMS reduces mortality**. Neonatal and perinatal mortality show no
  effect at high certainty. Maternal mortality is a wide, uninformative null (RR 1.06, 0.72–1.54)
  that Cochrane did not grade and WHO graded low certainty. MMS increases birth size; it does not
  demonstrably improve survival.
- **Daily iron-folic acid remains WHO's recommended standard of care.** MMS is recommended in the
  context of rigorous research.

## Opportunities in government adoption

- Substitute MMS for iron-folic acid in routine antenatal care.
- Secure inclusion on the **national essential medicines list** — the single highest-value
  advocacy step.
- Strengthen stock management. Implementation research across four countries found sustaining
  MMS supply "one of the most challenging aspects" of the programme as a whole.
- Measure **adherence across the pregnancy** rather than attendance at first antenatal contact.

*An asset worth using:* women report preferring MMS to iron-folic acid and experiencing fewer
side effects — the strongest adherence advantage identified in this review.

## Countries to focus

**South Asia** is the cleanest match: the only case in which absolute burden and relative benefit
point to the same geography. Beyond that, high-anaemia settings generally.

# 4. Breastfeeding support

## The problem

Global exclusive breastfeeding stands at 48% against a 2025 target of 50% and a 2030 target of
60% — close to the near-term target in aggregate, but with wide variation between and within
countries. Attrition concentrates in the months after birth.

## The solution

**A scheduled sequence of contacts, rather than a promotional message.** Across 116 trials,
breastfeeding support **probably reduces** cessation of exclusive breastfeeding at four to six
weeks and at six months (moderate certainty). Cochrane reports that an intermediate intensity —
four to eight visits — **may be** the most beneficial; this is the middle category of three, not
a demonstrated dose-response.

*Mechanism.* Breastfeeding fails for practical and tractable reasons — pain, perceived
insufficient supply, family pressure, return to work. These require repeated problem-solving at
the point they arise, which is why contact frequency predicts success and single-contact
messaging does not.

**Cadre is not the determinant.** Across 116 trials there was no differential effect by provider
— professional, peer or combined — though the review notes power was limited for that comparison.

**It works at scale, through either channel.** The Alive & Thrive programme raised exclusive
breastfeeding by **36 percentage points in Bangladesh and 28 in Viet Nam** — the Viet Nam
estimate falling to about 15 points once the authors adjust for social-desirability bias —
delivered through an NGO network in one country and government health facilities in the other.
The programme combined intensive counselling with mass media, community mobilisation and policy
advocacy; it was not counselling alone. No study randomised the channel, so this is suggestive
rather than proof that a government-anchored version performs equivalently.

## Caveats

- There is no evidence that breastfeeding support reduces neonatal mortality **in isolation**.
  The mortality evidence derives from multi-component packages — women's groups, home visits,
  birth-attendant training — in which breastfeeding support is one element. Its individual
  contribution cannot be separated.
- The distinction matters. The evidence on **improving breastfeeding practice** is robust; the
  evidence attributing a **mortality reduction** to breastfeeding support alone does not exist.
  Only the second is a genuine gap.

## Opportunities in government adoption

- Replace general breastfeeding promotion with a **specified contact schedule** in postnatal care.
- Select the cadre on cost and availability rather than on presumed effectiveness.
- Pair the facility moment — early initiation and skin-to-skin — with community follow-up. The
  two are complements: one establishes breastfeeding, the other sustains it to six months.

## Countries to focus

Less geographically determined than the others; this follows the health workforce rather than the
burden. Bangladesh and Viet Nam are the proven models.
