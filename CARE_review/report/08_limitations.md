# 8. Limitations and open decisions

## 8.1 What this review does not cover

**Cost and cost-effectiveness.** Excluded by design from queries, extraction and synthesis. This was a deliberate sequencing choice — evidence first, cost second — and cost is the subject of a separate phase. It is nevertheless a real gap for a scaling decision, and it will bear unevenly: commodity-heavy interventions (RUTF for CMAM, tablets for MMS) are far more cost-sensitive than counselling-based ones. Two findings in this report are effectively cost findings in disguise and should be carried into that phase: OptiMA achieves non-inferior recovery with about 46% less RUTF, and reducing the dairy content of RUTF — the main cost driver — measurably worsens recovery.

**A country recommendation or ranking.** Section 4 describes what is known about individual countries and stops there. An earlier draft scored and ranked them; that was removed because the ordering proved sensitive to measurement choices with no evidential basis (§4.1). The decisive factors — fragility and security, partner operational footprint, government appetite, financing — are not in the corpus in any case.

**Complementary feeding.** Both CARE technical experts advocated pairing counselling with a food or nutrient component. It was scoped out to hold the review to three interventions. **This needs a decision from partners**, since it was raised independently by two reviewers and sits adjacent to the breastfeeding community package.

**652 abstract-only papers remain unextracted.** All 981 full-text papers are complete, so the full-text evidence base is closed; the remainder would add breadth rather than depth. The practical effect is that findings resting on a single small study may be under-represented, while the meta-analytic backbone is unaffected.

## 8.2 Limits of the evidence base

**The active-control ceiling on CMAM.** Almost no trial compares treatment to no treatment, because that is no longer ethical. The measured benefit therefore understates the true benefit. This is a property of the literature, not a judgment on the intervention — and it is one reason this report no longer assigns comparative grades (§2.5).

**Geographic concentration.** CMAM evidence concentrates in sub-Saharan Africa; breastfeeding evidence remains high-income-skewed in the skin-to-skin and at-scale reviews, with only 17.4% of 115 at-scale reviews being LMIC-only. LMIC-specific estimates are flagged separately wherever the sources permitted separation.

**Fidelity is rarely documented.** The contact-dose finding for breastfeeding — that 4–8 postnatal contacts outperform fewer — rests on trials of which only 5 of 17 reported the contacts participants actually received. The target can be specified; adherence to it is largely unverified.

**Attribution in bundled interventions.** The neonatal-mortality benefit of postnatal home visits (RR 0.76) comes from packages delivering breastfeeding support alongside other newborn care. The breastfeeding-specific share cannot be isolated. Kangaroo Mother Care is cleaner, since skin-to-skin contact and breastfeeding are its mechanism.

**Country tags are imperfect.** 582 of 648 on-topic records carry a usable country tag; 36 carry only regional or pooled descriptors and 30 none. Multi-country reviews are reported separately from country-specific studies precisely because they distort country-level comparison (§4.2), which means the country-specific evidence base is smaller than raw record counts imply.

## 8.3 Limits of the method

This review was produced by an AI-assisted pipeline: automated retrieval, structured per-study extraction, grounded synthesis, and automated verification. Its distinctive claim is traceability — every number ties to a specific record — and that claim should be understood precisely.

**What verification establishes:** that a cited number appears in the record cited, and that no number in the report is untraceable to the corpus.

**What it does not establish:** that the underlying study is correct; that a pooled estimate is well-constructed; that the interpretation drawn from a finding is the only reasonable one; or that the retrieval found everything relevant. A number can be faithfully transcribed from a flawed source.

**Known specific risks.** Retrieval draws on PubMed and OpenAlex; grey literature, government programme documentation and NGO evaluations are under-represented, which matters disproportionately for implementation questions. Screening for on-topic status was model-performed. Cross-review trial overlap is captured for individual reviews — the twin-publication problem in Chapter 5 was caught this way — but systematic detection of double-counting across reviews is not yet implemented, so where several reviews pool overlapping trials, apparent corroboration may be partly illusory.

**Where the method demonstrably helped.** Full-text extraction surfaced the WHO technical correction that overturned the MMS home-birth safety signal, which an abstract-level read would have missed and which an earlier draft had treated as near-disqualifying. It also identified the twin publication of one 42-study evidence base as two apparently independent reviews.

## 8.4 Open decisions for partners

1. **Does complementary feeding come back into scope?** Raised independently by two CARE technical experts. Adding it would extend the review; leaving it out means the behavioural package addresses breastfeeding without the food component partners asked to pair with it.
2. **Country pre-commitments or exclusions.** Are there countries already ruled in or out for reasons outside this evidence base? That would materially change how §4 should be read.
3. **Scope of the co-design — one intervention or a package?** The evidence supports a combined design (all three run through the same CHW platform), but a single-intervention focus would change which countries are most relevant. Nepal, for instance, has a thin malnutrition-treatment evidence base but the strongest supplementation track record in the corpus.
4. **Emergency contexts.** Development settings were treated as primary. Partners flagged emergency-specific gaps in both breastfeeding and CMAM; the humanitarian evidence retrieved here (the Niger simplified-protocol results, for instance) is tagged but not developed as a separate analysis.
5. **Timing of the cost phase.** Whether cost-effectiveness runs before or after the country decision.
